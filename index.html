The Netflix Exit Play

Did Susan Rice's inflammatory remarks hand Netflix a $2.8 billion golden parachute and the perfect excuse to walk away from Warner Bros. Discovery?

Compiled February 23, 2026 — The day Paramount's "best and final" offer is due
I.

The Core Thesis

Netflix's stock has been punished since it announced its bid to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery for $82.7 billion in December 2025. The market never liked this deal. Meanwhile, Paramount Skydance has been waging a hostile campaign to buy all of WBD at a higher per-share price. The thesis: Netflix may be engineering the conditions for its own deal to fail — and profiting handsomely from the collapse.

Central Question

Could Susan Rice's provocative podcast comments — made at the most sensitive possible moment in the WBD bidding process — be part of a broader dynamic (whether orchestrated or tolerated) that gives Netflix political cover to walk away, collect a $2.8 billion breakup fee, and regain shareholder value?

V.

The Theory: Strategic Inaction

The strongest version of this thesis is not that Rice's comments were a coordinated sabotage scheme — the legal risks of active conspiracy would be enormous. Rather, it is a theory of strategic inaction.

Sarandos is a shrewd operator who built his career on calculated bets. He knows who Susan Rice is. He knows what she's likely to say on a podcast with Preet Bharara. He knows the WBD deal is increasingly problematic for Netflix's stock and faces mounting regulatory headwinds from a Trump DOJ.

If he wanted to create conditions where WBD might choose Paramount instead — triggering the breakup fee and relieving the stock — he wouldn't need to orchestrate anything. He'd simply need to not prevent Rice from being Rice. No phone call saying "Susan, please be careful right now." No board-level guidance about the sensitivity of the regulatory moment.

That kind of strategic inaction would be virtually impossible to prove, would carry none of the legal risk of active sabotage, and would be consistent with someone who built his career on letting situations play out while positioning himself to benefit from outcomes.

VI.

How the WBD Decision Works

The decision is a multi-step process involving both the WBD board and shareholders:

Step 1 — Board as Gatekeeper: The WBD board evaluates Paramount's offer and determines whether it constitutes a "superior proposal." They currently unanimously back Netflix but have a fiduciary duty to maximize shareholder value.

Step 2 — Netflix Matching Rights: If the board deems Paramount's offer superior, Netflix has 4 business days to match. If Netflix matches, the existing deal stays. If Netflix declines, the board can change its recommendation.

Step 3 — Shareholder Vote: A special meeting is scheduled for March 20. Shareholders of record as of Feb 4 vote on the merger. The board recommends, but shareholders have the final say.

Step 4 — Regulatory Approval: Whichever deal proceeds still requires DOJ approval, European Commission clearance, UK CMA approval, and numerous other international regulatory sign-offs.

VII.

Regulatory Landscape

Favoring Paramount

Trump admin alignment: Ellison family ties, Delrahim as CLO, political favor.

HSR cleared: Paramount's DOJ waiting period expired Feb 19. Netflix is still responding to its second request.

Easier antitrust story: Two legacy studios merging raises fewer streaming concentration concerns than Netflix + HBO Max.

Paramount argument: Their deal is "pro-competitive" — more theatrical releases, stronger Hollywood.

Risks for Paramount

International regulators (EU, UK CMA) operate independently of Trump and may scrutinize heavily.

Foreign financing from Saudi, Qatari, and UAE sovereign wealth funds could trigger national security reviews.

Political reversal: Democratic senators are already demanding Ellison preserve records of Trump admin contacts. Midterm shift could reopen scrutiny.

Massive leverage: The combined entity would carry enormous debt. Credit markets can shift.

The U.S. regulatory path clearly favors Paramount under this administration. But "easier" does not mean "certain," and international clearances could add 6-12 months of uncertainty — which is why WBD won't trade at the full offer price even after a deal is struck.

Rice's comments detonated at literally the worst possible moment if Netflix actually wants this deal — or at the most convenient possible moment if they'd prefer it to fall apart.

The Tell

Watch whether Netflix makes any effort to distance itself from Rice's comments — or quietly does nothing. Silence would be the most interesting signal for this thesis. As of this writing, Netflix has declined to comment on Trump's demand to fire Rice.

X.

Arguments Against This Thesis

Legal exposure: If it ever emerged that Rice's comments were coordinated, it could constitute bad faith dealing under the merger agreement — potentially jeopardizing the breakup fee or exposing Netflix to litigation and SEC scrutiny.

Rice's track record: She has a decades-long pattern of exactly this kind of blunt, politically charged rhetoric. Diplomats called her "rude." She withdrew from Secretary of State consideration rather than moderate her positions. The simplest explanation is that she was being herself.

Board independence: Rice is a board member, not an executive. She doesn't have the operational involvement that would make her a natural participant in M&A strategy. Board members have independent fiduciary duties.

Reputational risk: Future M&A counterparties might be wary of negotiating with a company perceived as engaging in this kind of gamesmanship.

The "strategic inaction" version is unfalsifiable: There's no way to prove someone didn't make a phone call. This makes the theory compelling as narrative but weak as evidence.

Conclusion

A $2.8 Billion Coincidence?

The most likely explanation remains the simplest: Susan Rice said what Susan Rice always says, without regard for corporate consequences. But the alignment of incentives, timing, and relationships is unusually precise.

Netflix gains nothing by winning a bidding war that its own shareholders don't want, that faces a hostile regulatory environment, and that would burden the company with massive integration costs. It gains everything by walking away with $2.8 billion, a recovered stock price, and the narrative of disciplined capital allocation.

Whether by design or by fortune, the pieces are falling Netflix's way. The next four days will tell us everything.
